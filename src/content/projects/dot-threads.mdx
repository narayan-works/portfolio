    <p>
        Research doesn't happen in folders. It happens in webs of connected ideas. Dot Threads is a browser extension that transforms web browsing into spatial knowledge building. Instead of burying research in folders, users create visual webs where every link connects by context.
    </p>

<ProjectSection title="The Problem" id="problem">

<div class="story-time">
<p>
    <strong>Short story time!</strong> I was researching embodied interfaces for my thesis when I found a paper called <em>Emoto: AI Sidekick</em>. The project caught my attention, so I started looking into the people behind it.
</p>
<p>
    Three of the authors were from CMU, and two of them were from the School of Design. That already felt close to my world, so I kept following the trail. I found that two of them later joined Experiments with Google, then eventually started their own company. Another author moved to Apple as a prototyper. The professor on the paper later moved to Northumbria University, started a lab, and worked on Spooky Technology, a project I recognized because one of its collaborators, Daragh Byrne, was someone I had worked with at CMU.
</p>
<p>
    At that point, I was not just saving a paper anymore. I was finding a small web of people, projects, labs, companies, and overlaps with my own work. If I came across someone from Experiments with Google later, I would want to remember this whole trail, not just the original link.
</p>
</div>
<hr class="feature-divider" />
<h3>But for me that's <span class="accent-text">12 open tabs</span>, <span class="accent-text">4 folders deep</span>, and <span class="accent-text">15 bookmarks</span> to sort through before you even get back to what you were thinking about.</h3>

<FullWidthImage src="/assets/projects/dot-threads/old bookmarking.png" height="400px" caption="Traditional folder-based bookmarking lists" constrain={false} />

    <h2 class="left-align">Validating the Problem</h2>
    <p class="left-align half-width">To see if there is a broader pattern with this problem, I surveyed 25 people across 3 CMU departments (Architecture, HCI, Design) to collect qualitative insights on how people organize their digital bookmarks.</p>

<h2 class="left-align">The insight</h2>
<p class="left-align half-width">Bookmarking systems break when the list gets too long. People either stop using them or forget where things were saved. Sharing adds more friction, pushing people into tools like Are.na, OneTab, or Notion and splitting the workflow across too many places.</p>

<div class="centered-tagline orange-bar gaps-container">
    <div class="gaps-title">So, here are two gaps:</div>
    <hr class="gaps-divider" />
    <div class="gaps-list">
        <div class="gap-item">
            <h4 class="gap-heading">☝️ High cognitive load</h4>
            <p class="gap-description">Overwhelming organizational choices at the point of saving.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="gap-item">
            <h4 class="gap-heading">✌️ Fragmented context</h4>
            <p class="gap-description">No preservation of relationships between related bookmarks.</p>
        </div>
    </div>
</div>
